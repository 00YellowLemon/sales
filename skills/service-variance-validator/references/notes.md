# Notes: How to Build an AI-Native Services Company

## The Market Opportunity
- Highlights that the next decade's biggest companies will be AI-native services companies (e.g., in insurance, law, tax, audit, healthcare, logistics), rebuilt from scratch with AI doing the majority of the work.
- Explains that these companies deliver an outcome directly to the customer rather than selling a co-pilot software for the customer to use internally.
- Discusses ideal market traits: low trust (work is already outsourced), high cost, high complexity, and where regulation sets a high bar that acts as a competitive moat.
- Advises against markets involving physical equipment or onsite labor due to a lack of software margin math.

## The Founding Team
- Notes three critical traits for founders in this space: domain fluency (direct or learned credibility), model fluency (understanding frontier model capabilities to ride the curve of improvement), and operational rigor.
- Emphasizes the need to understand operational concepts like throughput, cycle times, variance, and Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs).

## Building the Product and Handling Variance
- States that in AI services, the human is the interface to the customer, and the product is the internal software that helps the human scale non-linearly.
- Introduces variance (non-uniform outputs) as an existential threat; customers will fire a service for inconsistency faster than for being slow or expensive.
- Recommends tracking throughput and cycle times as core product metrics, similar to tracking daily active users in traditional software.
- Stresses the need to find and build for bottlenecks, using humans where the work genuinely needs judgment, not just to paper over product shortcomings.

## Pricing and Economics
- Warns against the "early demand trap": acquiring too many pilot customers too fast, which overwhelms service capacity and traps the company into using unscalable human labor.
- Advises capping pilots, learning where AI provides unique leverage, and selling outcomes rather than seats or tokens.
- Recommends outcome-based or per-unit pricing (e.g., per return, per claim) while explicitly warning against cost-plus pricing and straight-line undercutting.
- Discusses the P&L focus: obsessing over Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)—comprising model costs, hosting, and human-in-the-loop labor—to achieve "AI operating leverage," aiming for near-software margins (50%+) in markets much larger than pure software.
- Concludes by advising against trying to buy legacy service businesses to layer AI on top, as they possess different expectations, metrics, and cultures; building from scratch is almost always better.
