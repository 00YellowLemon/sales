# B2B Pricing Strategy & Sales Execution

## 1. Introduction: The Founder's Pricing Dilemma
* **The Common Problem:** Founders often freeze when a successful sales call leads to a prospect (the "Champion") asking for pricing.
* **Lack of Calibration:** Founders, especially those without enterprise experience, lack calibration for what companies pay for software. They often default to consumer-level pricing (e.g., $19 or $49/month) based on their own subscriptions like GitHub or ChatGPT.
* **The Discomfort:** Asking for tens or hundreds of thousands of dollars feels deeply uncomfortable for a founder who has only spent a few months building a product.

## 2. The Core Element: The Value Equation
* **The Most Important Metric:** The "Value Equation" is the most critical element of pricing. If you do this correctly, you will get 80-90% of your pricing spot on.
* **The Process:** Sit down with the internal Champion and clearly write out exactly what value the product will deliver to their company.
* **Types of Value:** Value typically falls into three categories:
  1. Direct cost savings.
  2. Time savings (which translates to cost savings).
  3. Direct revenue increases.
* **Validation:** Have the customer actively challenge and poke holes in your assumptions. This solidifies the math.
* **The Goal:** This equation becomes the exact tool the Champion will use to justify the ROI to their boss or CFO to get the contract approved.
* **An Example Breakdown:**
  * Imagine selling an AI customer service tool to a 100-person support team.
  * *Cost per agent:* $50,000 salary + $50,000 overhead (fully loaded cost = $100,000).
  * *Total department cost:* $10 million/year.
  * *The Product's Value:* The AI tool eliminates 20% of queries (saving 20% of total time).
  * *Total Value Delivered:* $2 million in cost savings.
* **Calculating the Price:** Do not charge for the full value. A strong rule of thumb is to charge between 25% to 33% (one-third) of the total value delivered.
  * *Example Pricing:* If you deliver $2 million in value, the customer keeps ~$1.3 million, and you charge ~$700,000. This is a massive win for both parties.
* **Pilots & Success Metrics:** The value equation explicitly defines the success metrics for a pilot program. If you claim a 20% time reduction, the pilot (e.g., testing with 10 agents for one month) must prove that specific 20% metric. If the metric fluctuates (e.g., 15% or 25%), you can adjust the final contract price accordingly.

## 3. The Second Element: Cost as a Floor
* **Cost-Plus Pricing is a Trap:** Never start pricing based on costs (cost-plus-margin pricing). This virtually guarantees you will severely underprice the software. Cost should only ever act as a floor.
* **The Ideal Margin:** Aim for 80% to 90% software gross margins.
* **Running the Math:**
  * Contract Value (from Value Equation): $700,000.
  * Product Costs (AWS, OpenAI API fees, etc.): $200,000.
  * *Result:* Golden. You have a highly sustainable, high-margin business.
  * *The Warning Sign:* If the 1/3 value share is $150,000, but your costs are $200,000, you are in a fundamentally bad business and must pivot, increase value, or quit.
* **Handling Startup Credits:** Treat free infrastructure credits (AWS, OpenAI, Microsoft) as actual cash costs when calculating margins. Do not assume credits will last forever; ignoring them will destroy your financial model.
* **Pricing Below Cost:** Occasionally, founders price at or below cost as a strategic land-grab to capture market share. This is a highly dangerous maneuver. While LLM costs (OpenAI, Anthropic) are continually dropping—offering some argument for aggressive future-proofing—it remains incredibly risky. Stick to the 80-90% margin goal.

## 4. The Third Element: Handling Competition
* **The Scenario:** You have a sustainable, value-based price, but a direct competitor enters the market with an identical product priced at half your rate.
* **The Wrong Move:** Do not engage in a price war. Undercutting leads to a reciprocal race to the bottom, destroying all margins (similar to the airline industry, which survives on a brutal 2.7% margin because seats are a pure commodity).
* **The Right Move:** Never allow an apples-to-apples comparison. Differentiate the product immediately based on unique functionality, hyper-specific industry focus, or unique integrations so the products become incomparable.

## 5. Determining Pricing Structure through Customer Habits
* **Understanding Buying Patterns:** Ask the Champion what pricing structures they typically pay for when buying similar software.
* **Common Structures:** Monthly flat fees, per-seat pricing, usage bands, or credits.
* **Mirroring Strategy:** Explore the specific industry you are selling into. It is critical to adopt a pricing structure the customer is already accustomed to.
* **Handling Usage-Based Models:** Buyers are typically very wary of entirely uncapped usage-based pricing. It is strongly advised to implement caps to alleviate these fears. Overall, mirroring familiar software purchasing models yields the best results.

## 6. Simplicity is Paramount
* **The Golden Rule:** Keep pricing simple.
* **The Risk:** Overcomplicating the pricing model will actively kill a sales process.

## 7. Recurring Revenue vs. Usage-Based Pricing
* **Preference for Committed Revenue:** Committed Monthly Recurring Revenue (MRR) or Annual Recurring Revenue (ARR) is vastly superior to pure usage-based pricing.
* **Economic Resilience:** During an economic downturn, committed revenue protects the business at least until the contract renewal period, allowing for a debate on value before churn. Conversely, usage-based revenue can simply "fall off a cliff" during a bad month, a scenario that terrifies investors.
* **Transition Strategy (The "Bait and Switch" to ARR):**
  * Start new customers on a usage-based model for the first 1-2 months to gauge their actual volume.
  * Once usage is established (e.g., they average $15,000/month), offer to transition them to a committed minimum monthly flat fee with volume discounts.
  * *Example:* Offer a $12,000/month flat fee that covers all their current usage, contingent on them signing a 12-month contract.

## 8. Pricing for Pilot Programs
* **Identifying Signing Authority:** Directly ask your Champion what dollar amount they are personally authorized to sign off on without needing secondary approvals from the CFO or legal department.
* **Strategic Pilot Pricing:** Use their signing limit to dictate the price of the pilot program.
  * *Example:* If the Champion can approve up to $15,000, price the pilot at $14,999.
* **The Goal:** This tactic bypasses enterprise bureaucracy and keeps the sales process moving incredibly fast.

## 9. Website Pricing vs. "Contact Sales"
* **The Debate:** Software developers often hate "Contact Sales" buttons, preferring transparent pricing to just swipe a credit card. However, public enterprise pricing is highly discouraged.
* **The Problem with Public Enterprise Pricing:** The "value equation" fluctuates wildly from one enterprise to another. Publishing a static price guarantees you will:
  * Overprice the software for a segment that gets lower value (losing them entirely).
  * Dramatically underprice the software for the segment getting massive value (leaving substantial money on the table).
* **The Strategy:**
  * Offer one or two inexpensive, transparent plans (e.g., individual, startup, or small team tiers) with basic functionality.
  * Gate enterprise-specific core functionality behind a "Contact Sales" tier.
* **Enterprise Gated Features:** Limit features like SOC 2 audit reports, Single Sign-On (SSO), audit logs, compliance reporting, and data geographic residency to the enterprise tier. Small businesses don't care about these, but enterprises find them absolutely vital.
* **The Result:** This gating strategy allows you to charge up to 10x more for enterprise customers based strictly on compliance, legal, and privacy features.

## 10. Aligning Pricing Strategy with Sales Channels
* **The Core Question:** Does your contract size contain enough margin to compensate a dedicated Account Executive (AE) or sales team?
* **The 5:1 Rule of Thumb:** There should be a 5:1 ratio between new signed ARR and the salesperson's total compensation (base salary + commission).
* **Math Breakdown (Assuming $100k Total Comp -> $500k ARR Quota):**
  * **Whale Hunting:** Five $100,000 contracts/year. The AE works on only 4-6 deals simultaneously.
  * **Mid-Market:** Twenty $25,000 contracts/year (approx. 2 deals/month). Highly doable for a traditional AE.
  * **SMB / High Volume:** $1,000 contracts/year ($83/month). This requires closing 500 deals/year (about 2 deals every single working day).
* **Channel Implication:** The high-volume ($1,000/yr) scenario cannot support an outbound AE model. It necessitates an inbound "call center" or inside sales model where reps merely harvest existing demand and process orders.

## 11. Structuring Free Trials and Pilots
* **The Trap:** Offering lengthy free trials or pilots is counterproductive because the customer has no real "buy-in" or skin in the game.
* **Best Practice for POCs:** Keep Proof of Concepts (POCs) incredibly short—maximum 2 to 4 weeks—with rigorously defined success criteria tied directly to the value equation.
* **The Alternative ("The Opt-Out"):** Push the customer to sign a full annual contract on day one, but include a 30-day or 60-day "money-back guarantee" or opt-out clause.
  * *Benefit:* If it fails, they get a refund with no questions asked. However, by default, it registers as recurring revenue immediately.

## 12. Positioning as a Startup
* **Faking Size:** A common mistake for 2-3 person startups is trying to inflate their size (via fake website profiles or LinkedIn padding) to appear like a large corporation to close enterprise deals. This is a bad idea.
* **Playing to Startup Strengths:** Lean into the reality of being small.
* **The Pitch:** Promise direct access. Tell the customer, "You have the cell phone numbers of the founders, and we are on call 24/7 to fix your problems."
* **The Differentiator:** Remind them they will never receive that level of bespoke, immediate attention from giants like Salesforce or Oracle.

## 13. The "I Don't Know How to Price" Strategy
* **The Baseline:** If the value equation fails or feels too uncertain, pick an arbitrary number that aligns with what customers pay for similar software categories.
* **The 50% Rule for Iteration:** Increase the quoted price by 50% for every subsequent new customer pitch.
  * *Example:* Pitch $10,000 to Customer A. If they say yes, pitch $15,000 to Customer B. If they say yes, pitch $22,000 to Customer C.
* **The Ceiling:** Stop aggressively raising the price when you begin losing more than 25% of your potential deals *based solely on the price*. At that point, you have found the correct ballpark.
* **Winning Too Much:** If you are winning every single deal, you are fundamentally underpricing the product.
* **The Long Game:** Don't obsess over early pricing. The first 5-10 customers represent a microscopic fraction of the revenue the company will generate over 5 years. Getting deals flowing is more important than optimization.
* **Future Leverage:** Prices can always be raised later via upselling, adding new paywalled modules, and as the product naturally improves. Sales velocity in the early days matters more than margin.

## 14. Final Recap: The Three Pillars of Pricing
* **1. Value Equation:** Write it down, make the Champion challenge it, and price the product at approximately 1/3 of the total value delivered (meaning the customer retains 2/3 of the value).
* **2. Cost Considerations:** Never price at or below the cost of delivery unless there is a strictly defined roadmap to dramatically reduce costs in the near term.
* **3. Competition:** Avoid pricing wars at all costs; they are a race to the bottom. Instead, differentiate the product by leaning into specific niches, building unique integrations, or targeting hyper-specific industries to make the product completely incomparable to competitors.
