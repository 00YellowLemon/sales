# Enterprise Sales for Software Startups
*Based on Y Combinator's Startup School talk by Pete Koomen (YC Group Partner, co-founder and CTO of Optimizely).*

## Introduction
- **Goal:** Walk step-by-step through the process of closing your first Enterprise customers.
- **Funnel Stages Covered:** Prospecting, Outreach, Qualification, Pricing, Closing, and Implementation.
- **Focus:** Enterprise sales for software startups, but broadly useful to any founder getting started with sales regardless of customer size or what they are selling.
- **Why this matters:** Sales is the number one concern for most founders during the YC batch.
- **Core Lesson:** Sales is a **learnable skill**. Pete and his co-founder Dan were technical founders who knew how to build a product but didn't know how to get people to use it. They figured it out through trial and error.

## The Founder's Role in Sales
- If you are building a product you hope other businesses will buy, **you are capable of selling it**.
- You are likely the *only* person capable of selling your product at first. If you can't sell it yourself initially, chances are you won't be able to hire someone else to do it.
- **Why not just hire a salesperson?** Sales *before* Product-Market Fit (PMF) is fundamentally different from sales *after* PMF.
  - Pre-PMF sales is highly **entrepreneurial**. It requires:
    - Vision.
    - Credibility with customers.
    - Lots of experimentation.
    - A tight feedback loop with the people building the product.
  - This is a role specifically for founders. You do not necessarily need a business co-founder to sell.
- **Technical Founder Advantages in Sales:**
  1. **Expertise:** You are an expert in both the problem you are solving and the product you are building.
  2. **Conviction:** You sincerely believe the product will solve the customer's problem.
  - *Nuance:* Expertise and conviction are surprisingly important. People mistakenly think selling is a "dark art full of psychological tricks." In reality, sales isn't about tricking people; it's fundamentally about helping people solve their problems—something engineers are great at doing anyway.

## Step 1: Prospecting
- **Definition:** Finding potential customers.
- **Output:** A list of companies you think might need your product, and the specific humans at those companies you think might buy it.
- **The Sales Hypothesis:** Before starting, you need a hypothesis.
  - *Format:* "Customer X has problem Y, and our product will help them solve it."
  - *Example (Optimizely):* "Marketers at small and medium tech, media, and e-commerce companies want to run A/B tests on their websites, but they can't because off-the-shelf experimentation tools require users to write code. Optimizely will enable them to run A/B tests without writing code."
  - *Benefit:* A clear hypothesis makes prospecting easy by clarifying exactly who you should be talking to.
- **Finding Companies:**
  - Identify companies likely to suffer from the problem.
  - Buy industry lists of companies in a given sector.
  - Use filtering criteria to qualify companies and narrow the target list.
  - *Example (Optimizely):* Used a tool called **BuiltWith** to figure out whether prospects were using analytics tools and JavaScript frameworks. These were signals that a company was relatively sophisticated and cared about their website.
- **Finding Humans:**
  - Once you have the list of companies, find the right humans and their contact information.
  - *Tools mentioned:* **Apollo** and **LinkedIn Sales Navigator**.

## Step 2: Outreach
- **Goal:** Get the prospect's attention and schedule a meeting (a product demo or first call).
- **Inbound Demand (The easiest way):**
  - Even if you plan on using a sales-led approach, do everything you can to generate inbound demand (getting them to reach out to you).
  - Launch early and often.
  - Create technical content (videos, blog posts) prospects can find while searching for a solution.
  - Build self-serve demos that people can share.
  - Find online forums where your customers hang out and establish yourself as an expert by answering questions.
  - **Conferences:** If customers hang out at industry conferences, be there. Get the attendee list ahead of time and set up lots of meetings in advance.
- **Outbound / Cold Outreach:**
  - Start by trying to find a **warm introduction** (look on LinkedIn for shared connections and ask for an intro).
  - Cold emails are usually the least efficient way to get attention, but can be effective if done right.
  - *Tactics for Cold Emails:*
    1. Write each email by hand.
    2. Make emails short and to the point.
    3. Make the "ask" clear.
    4. Make it clear *why* you are reaching out to this specific recipient (humans have built-in spam filters; if it looks like it was sent to thousands of people, it gets deleted).
  - *Golden Rule:* **Only send emails that you yourself would be excited to read.** If you wouldn't be excited to get it, your prospect probably won't either.
- **The Major Anti-Pattern: Talking to Anyone Who Will Take Your Call**
  - Problem: This selects for people who are *easiest* to talk to, not the people who will be *great customers*.
  - You end up wasting all your time chasing bad customers because cold emailing is a demoralizing grind, making it tempting to go after anyone who will listen.
  - *Danger:* Talking to bad customers gives you the **illusion of progress** when you're not making any. You get product feedback from people who think they are doing you a favor, but because they don't actually need your product, the feedback is "useless at best and counterproductive at worst."
  - *Two common manifestations of this mistake:*
    1. **Selling enterprise software to startups:** If your product solves a problem companies only have when they get big (e.g., an HR information system), selling to startups is a waste of time. Founders do it anyway because startups are easier to talk to than busy big-company executives.
    2. **Going bottom-up with a product that needs to be adopted top-down:**
       - Bottom-up (like Notion) works if individual employees/teams can adopt it independently.
       - Top-down (like software for large hospitals) requires coordination across many teams (CIO for security/compliance, software team for integration, doctors to enter codes, ops team for collections). Talking to an individual doctor is useless; you must talk to a senior leader (CFO/CIO) to do a deal.
  - *Correction on a Meme:* YC says "sell to companies who will buy quickly." This is often misconstrued. You *should* find companies that buy quickly, but you *shouldn't* spend time trying to sell to companies that don't actually need your product or won't be good customers. They must have the problem, the budget, and the decision-making authority.

## Step 3: Qualification (The First Call)
- **Primary Rule:** Your job on the first call is **NOT to sell your product**. Do not dive straight into your pitch.
- **Goals of the First Call:**
  1. **Qualify the Prospect:** Figure out whether they have the problem you're trying to solve, the budget, and the decision-making authority to buy the product.
  2. **Schedule a follow-up call** for a product demo (if qualified).
- **The Mindset Shift on Sales:**
  - Founders often view the target company as a "big monolithic entity" and the sales process as "adversarial" (where the goal is a perfect pitch to break down defenses).
  - *Reality:* You are almost always selling to an *individual human*, not a monolith. Humans are easier to understand.
  - Sales is not adversarial. It's about deeply understanding a customer's problem and helping them solve it.
- **Listen > Pitch:** Great salespeople spend most of their time listening, as it is the best way to understand the problem.
- **Crucial Qualification Questions (Scripting Nuances):**
  - *What made you decide to take this call?*
  - *Tell me about this problem.*
  - *How long have you had it?*
  - *How bad is it?*
  - *Who else does it affect?*
  - *How do you quantify the impact?*
  - *Why haven't you solved it already?*
  - *What's your budget for solving it?*
  - *How does your organization buy software?*
  - *Who makes the buying decision?*
  - *Who else will need to weigh in on this decision?*
- **Outcome:** Sometimes you discover they don't have the problem, don't care enough to solve it, or lack budget. *This is a great outcome.* You just saved both parties time and can focus energy on better prospects. If they do have a solvable problem, you've earned the opportunity to show them how your product works.

## Step 4: Delivering a Great Demo
- **Mindset Shift:** A demo is not a chance to show off your product. It is a chance to convince the audience that you can help them solve their problem.
- **The "Great Movie Script" Approach:**
  - Think of the demo as the script for a great movie.
  - Start by recapping who the main character is (the user) and the problem she is trying to solve.
  - This demonstrates how well you were listening during the first call. If the audience believes you understand their company and problems, they will take you seriously when you talk about solutions.
- **Avoid the Feature Tour:** Do not walk screen to screen showing everything the product can do.
- **Tell a Story:** Show exactly how the main character solves her problem. Demos should have a flow where each step leads to the next, and every feature shown has a clear reason for being there.
- **Magic Moments:** Surprise your audience with how easy or delightful something is.
- **Personalization is Critical:**
  - Use the information collected during the first call.
  - Tailor the demo to their company: Use their logo, their website, their customers, and the names of the people on their team. Help them visualize exactly how the product would work in *their* company.
  - *Example (Optimizely):* Instead of using a dummy website to show A/B testing (which competitors did and was "lame"), they spent weeks building a feature that let them demo the product directly on the *customer's live website*. Marketers' eyes "lit up" when they saw things changed on their own landing page that would have taken months to do on their own.

## Step 5: Pricing
- **No Simple Formula:** There isn't a formula. Pricing involves a lot of guessing in the beginning.
- **Leverage Qualification Questions:** Use the answers from the first call to guide pricing:
  - *How much is this problem costing your company?*
  - *How many people maintain your in-house solution?*
  - *What's your budget?*
  - *How much are you spending on my competitor?*
- **When to Wait:** If your product requires lots of work or customization to implement, do *not* quote a price until you fully understand exactly what the customer needs.
- **Pricing as an Experiment:** Treat each pricing conversation as an opportunity to test a price point and learn from the prospect's reaction.
  - *Tactic (Optimizely):* They published self-serve pricing for a basic version, but left the "Enterprise plan" pricing unpublished (requiring a sales call). This gave them the flexibility to try a different price each time.
- **The Biggest Pricing Mistake:** Charging too little, or making it free in exchange for product feedback, out of fear of scaring customers away.
- **The Reality of High Prices:**
  - When a customer *really* wants your product, it's hard to scare them away by quoting too high.
  - *Example:* Optimizely quoted a prospect $10,000/month. The prospect talked them down to $2,000/month and bought. Their initial quote was 5x what the prospect was willing to pay, and they still bought.
  - Higher prices help figure out if customers actually need your product. (e.g., the Collison brothers famously charged more for Stripe initially than competition. Selling it anyway proved they were onto something and focused them on the most desperate customers).
  - **High prices make customers more serious.**
- **Enable Your Champion for Internal Conversations:**
  - The most important pricing conversations happen *without you in the room*.
  - The prospect needs to convince others that the product is worth the asking price.
  - Make their job easier: Give them slides or a PDF one-pager that explains how pricing works, includes an overview of the product, and details the benefits (for stakeholders not familiar with the product).
- **Actionable Advice:** Don't overthink it. Pick a number—ideally one that makes you a little uncomfortable—and pay attention to how the prospect reacts. It's okay to let them negotiate you down. In the first few sales, **you are optimizing for learning, not unit economics.**

## Step 6: Closing the Deal
- **Definition:** Closing is not a single conversation. It is a bunch of things that need to happen from the moment the customer decides they want the product to the moment they actually buy it.
- **The Procurement Process:**
  - **Big companies (highly regulated):** Formal processes including security reviews, privacy reviews, legal reviews, and sign-off from compliance teams.
  - **Smaller companies:** Less formal, but expect at minimum a redlining process with their legal team.
- **The Biggest Mistake:** Getting surprised. Thinking a deal is "done" when it is not, and then discovering it will take weeks/months of back-and-forth or fall through completely.
- **How to Avoid Surprises:** Ask a lot of questions upfront.
  - *How do you buy software?*
  - *Who needs to sign off?*
  - This gives a clear picture of the hurdles required to get a signature.
- **Speeding up Procurement:**
  - Do everything you can to move quickly.
  - Ask explicitly if there are steps (like a security questionnaire) that you can start early and execute in parallel.
  - Keep legal documents as simple as possible. (Recommendation: Start with open-source templates published by YC company **Common Paper**).
  - Keep timelines and scope of work *out* of the legal contract if possible. Put them in an order form or a shared project tracking document instead.
- **Leverage Your Champion:**
  - Your prospect has now become your Champion. They are your biggest ally.
  - Be in constant communication with them.
  - When you need help getting something unstuck, ask them first. They can't solve their problem until you get through procurement, so they are heavily incentivized to help make it happen.

## Step 7: Managing the Implementation
- **The Single Biggest Mistake:** Thinking that implementation is the customer's job.
- *Example (Optimizely):* Closed a 6-figure deal with excited marketing customers. A year later, at renewal time, they hadn't run a single A/B test. Why? The marketing team couldn't convince the software engineering team to help them install it.
  - *The Real Cause:* The founders thought the customer was buying a *product*, so they sold it and left the rest up to them. In reality, the customer was buying a *solution* to a problem. All the work required to get from product to solution was the founder's responsibility.
- **Best Practices for Implementation:**
  - Start asking marketing/engineering leaders about the work required to implement *very early* in the sales process.
  - Build detailed implementation plans with those leaders well *before* a contract gets signed. (If you can't do that, don't sign a contract at all).
  - **Project manage it:** Treat the customer implementation the same way you would a high-priority project inside your own company.
  - Put together a shared roadmap.
  - Make sure every task has an owner.
  - Set up regular check-in meetings to hold everyone on both sides accountable for getting it done.
- **The True End of the Funnel:** Your sales funnel only really ends when your customer is using your product *habitually*. When you reach that point, you hopefully have a customer for life.
