# Enterprise Sales Prospecting Notes

*Based on Y Combinator's Startup School talk by Pete Koomen*

## Introduction
- **Goal:** Walk step-by-step through the process of closing your first Enterprise customers.
- **Why this matters:** Sales is the number one concern for most founders during the YC batch.
- **Core Lesson:** Sales is a **learnable skill**. Pete and his co-founder Dan were technical founders who knew how to build a product but didn't know how to get people to use it. They figured it out through trial and error.

## The Founder's Role in Sales
- If you are building a product you hope other businesses will buy, **you are capable of selling it**.
- You are likely the *only* person capable of selling your product at first. If you can't sell it yourself initially, chances are you won't be able to hire someone else to do it.
- **Why not just hire a salesperson?** Sales *before* Product-Market Fit (PMF) is fundamentally different from sales *after* PMF.
  - Pre-PMF sales is highly **entrepreneurial**. It requires vision, credibility with customers, lots of experimentation, and a tight feedback loop with the builders.
- **Technical Founder Advantages in Sales:**
  1. **Expertise:** You are an expert in both the problem you are solving and the product you are building.
  2. **Conviction:** You sincerely believe the product will solve the customer's problem.
  - Sales isn't about tricking people; it's fundamentally about helping people solve their problems.

## Step 1: Prospecting
- **Definition:** Finding potential customers.
- **Output:** A list of companies you think might need your product, and the specific humans at those companies you think might buy it.
- **The Sales Hypothesis:** Before starting, you need a hypothesis.
  - *Format:* "Customer X has problem Y, and our product will help them solve it."
  - *Example (Optimizely):* "Marketers at small and medium tech, media, and e-commerce companies want to run A/B tests on their websites, but they can't because off-the-shelf experimentation tools require users to write code. Optimizely will enable them to run A/B tests without writing code."
  - *Benefit:* A clear hypothesis makes prospecting easy by clarifying exactly who you should be talking to.
- **Finding Companies:**
  - Identify companies likely to suffer from the problem.
  - Buy industry lists of companies in a given sector.
  - Use filtering criteria to qualify companies and narrow the target list. (e.g., using BuiltWith to identify specific technology stacks).
- **Finding Humans:**
  - Once you have the list of companies, find the right humans and their contact information. Use tools like Apollo and LinkedIn Sales Navigator.

## Step 2: Outreach
- **Goal:** Get the prospect's attention and schedule a meeting (a product demo or first call).
- **Inbound Demand (The easiest way):**
  - Even if you plan on using a sales-led approach, do everything you can to generate inbound demand (getting them to reach out to you).
  - Launch early and often, create technical content, build self-serve demos, answer questions on forums.
  - **Conferences:** Set up lots of meetings in advance with attendees.
- **Outbound / Cold Outreach:**
  - Start by trying to find a **warm introduction**.
  - *Tactics for Cold Emails:*
    1. Write each email by hand.
    2. Make emails short and to the point.
    3. Make the "ask" clear.
    4. Make it clear *why* you are reaching out to this specific recipient.
  - *Golden Rule:* **Only send emails that you yourself would be excited to read.**
- **The Major Anti-Pattern: Talking to Anyone Who Will Take Your Call**
  - Problem: This selects for people who are *easiest* to talk to, not the people who will be *great customers*.
  - *Danger:* Talking to bad customers gives you the **illusion of progress**. You get useless or counterproductive feedback.
  - *Two common manifestations of this mistake:*
    1. **Selling enterprise software to startups:** If your product solves a problem only big companies have, startups are a waste of time, even if they are easier to talk to.
    2. **Going bottom-up with a product that needs to be adopted top-down:** If a product requires coordination across many teams, you must talk to a senior leader, not an individual contributor.
  - You *shouldn't* spend time trying to sell to companies that don't actually need your product or won't be good customers. They must have the problem, the budget, and the decision-making authority.
