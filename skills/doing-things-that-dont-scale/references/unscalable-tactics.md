# Unscalable Tactics Reference Guide

This document contains detailed definitions of specific unscalable tactics referenced in the `doing-things-that-dont-scale` agent skill. Use these to understand the specific historical context and execution of these strategies when advising founders.

## 1. The "Collison Installation"
Invented by the Collison brothers (founders of Stripe). When targeting other Y Combinator startups as early users, they bypassed the standard diffident approach ("Will you try our beta? Great, we'll send you a link."). Instead, when anyone agreed to try Stripe, they would say, "Right then, give me your laptop," and set them up on the spot.
**Why it works:** It guarantees activation, eliminates user friction during the most fragile stage of onboarding, and gives the founders immediate, over-the-shoulder feedback on the integration process.

## 2. Pulling a "Meraki"
A tactic for hardware startups to overcome the Catch-22 of needing capital for factory minimum order quantities (MOQs) but needing product growth to raise that capital. The founders of Meraki (and later, Pebble) got started by manually assembling their hardware (routers and watches, respectively) themselves.
**Why it works:** It generates the initial revenue and traction needed for crowdfunding or investment while teaching the founders critical lessons about supply chain and component sourcing (e.g., "how valuable it was to source good screws") before committing to a massive factory run.

## 3. The "Contained Fire" Strategy
A strategy particularly crucial for Marketplaces and Social Networks. Instead of launching broadly, you restrict the initial launch to a deliberately narrow subset of the market to get a critical mass quickly.
**Example:** Facebook starting exclusively for Harvard students.
**Why it works:** It creates intense density and makes the initial users feel the product is their "natural home." It's like keeping a fire contained in a small space at first to get it really hot before adding more logs.

## 4. The "Single-User Consultant" (B2B)
For B2B startups, acting as an unpaid consultant for a single early user. The founder builds the product acting as if they are building custom software just for that one user.
**Why it works:** The initial user serves as the form for the mold. By tweaking until it fits their needs perfectly, founders usually discover they have created something adjacent users want too. *Caveat:* The founder must not charge hourly consulting rates for this attentiveness, or the relationship changes from grateful early-adopter to demanding client.

## 5. Being the Software (Wizard of Oz MVP)
Instead of just using your own software on behalf of users (which is also good), the founders *are* the software. Doing things entirely manually behind the scenes that the user perceives as automated software.
**Example:** Stripe delivering "instant" merchant accounts by having founders manually sign users up for traditional merchant accounts behind the scenes.
**Why it works:** It allows for incredibly fast launches and guarantees that when the founders finally write the code to automate the process, they possess exact "muscle memory" of the bottleneck and know exactly what needs to be built.
