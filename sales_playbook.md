# The Sales Playbook For Founders | Startup School

## Section 1: The B2B Sales Progression and Common Pitfalls
*   **The Context:** At YC, many founders navigate B2B sales for the first time and make "common and easily avoidable mistakes."
*   **The Typical Progression:**
    1.  Poorly defined, overly long unpaid "design partnerships."
    2.  Free trials / pilots / proof of concepts.
    3.  Paid pilots (shorter, with financial commitment).
    4.  **The "Pro Move":** Rapid, well-defined sales processes resulting in recurring revenue contracts right off the bat (with opt-outs).
*   **The Goal:** Progress through this sequence as rapidly as possible to close new ARR every week.
*   **Where Founders Get Stuck:** "90% of the time, the vast vast majority of the time, founders get stuck in the very early stages," specifically in long unpaid design partnerships.
*   **The Outliers:** Rarely (5-10% of the time), founders try to "speedrun" to the end before their product is mature or without enough social proof, but "most founders are too slow."

## Section 2: The Trap of "Design Partnerships"
*   **When they happen:** Early in the journey (Figma mockups, barely any code) or when selling into complex industries (law, accounting) where the founder lacks domain knowledge.
*   **The Pitch:** Spend time observing a customer (often one with a "big fancy logo") to co-design the product.
*   **The Reality/Issues:**
    *   **Too Long:** They often last 3 to 6 months.
    *   **Poorly Defined:** Vague scope and meandering engagement.
    *   **Low Engagement:** Because the customer isn't paying and has a business to run, they don't spend much time with you.
    *   **The Vanity Metric:** The founder gets to put a fancy logo on their website, which "feels like progress" and makes them proud, but it brings them "no closer to real revenue."
*   **How Customers Exploit It:** Customers sometimes treat founders as an "unpaid dev shop." They provide detailed lists of bespoke requirements relevant only to their business.
    *   *Psychology:* The founders are "too meek to ask for money" and want to make the customer happy, doing bespoke work for free. It ends up "seeming like an abusive relationship."
*   **The "Nice Customer" Problem:** Instead of saying the product sucks, the design partner tries to be "helpful" by inventing "just one more feature" they might want. "They just don't want to hurt your feelings by saying no this sucks."

## Section 3: The Right Way to Do Early Customer Discovery
*   **The Value of Observation:** It *is* extremely valuable to sit next to a customer, observe their work, and look at their keyboard.
*   **The Discovery Questions:**
    *   *"What's the part of your job you hate the most?"*
    *   *"If you could wave a magic wand, what part of your work would you get rid of?"*
*   **Extreme Tactics:** Offer to do the work manually for them to understand it. "We've seen some of the best founders even go undercover and get qualified to work as an auditor or a real estate agent or an accountant and actually go and do the work... for a couple of months."
*   **The Wedge Strategy:**
    *   Identify a "really narrow burning problem."
    *   Build a "narrow wedge product" in as little as 48 hours.
    *   Bring it back, test it, and iterate until they absolutely love it.
*   **Don't Overbuild:** If they pay for the wedge, *do not* build more features for them. Take that same wedge and sell it to 10 similar customers.
    *   *The Mistake:* Overbuilding a broad platform to reach "feature parity" with existing software is a massive waste of resources for a small startup.
    *   *Advice:* Focus on doing one part of the solution really well. "More and more features are not the answer." Sell the wedge aggressively for a couple of weeks; if it fails, pick a different wedge.

## Section 4: Free Trials, Pilots, and Proof of Concepts (POCs)
*   **The Transition:** Founders move here once they realize design partnerships are a waste of time. These are used when the product exists but lacks social proof.
*   **The Problem:** Like design partnerships, they are often too long (2-3 months) and suffer from low commitment and no clear target/end goal.
    *   *Anecdote:* A founder in France was asked for a "pre-proof of concept," which the speaker notes as absurdly low commitment.
*   **Structuring a Proper POC:**
    *   You must understand: "What are you trying to prove?"
    *   Define the "Value Equation" with the customer (e.g., percentage savings or revenue uplift).
    *   *Example Scenario:* Selling customer service AI. The claim is solving 20% of inbound queries, reducing the team from 100 to 80, saving $1M/year. The software costs $200k.
    *   *The Proof:* Take a sample (e.g., 1,000 queries) and measure the actual resolution rate to prove the value equation.
    *   *The Outcome:* The internal champion takes this proven value equation to the CFO/CEO as a win-win investment ($200k for $1M value).
*   **Techniques for Cautious Buyers (Lowering Risk):**
    *   **Backtesting:** Run the product on historical data.
    *   **Side-by-Side Trials:** Run your AI alongside human agents without sending the AI answers to actual customers, then compare.
    *   **Volume Limiting:** Take on just 1% of total volume.
    *   **Geographic Rollout:** Start in a smaller geography before a wide release.
    *   *The Goal:* Ensure the champion is "not going to get fired if it all goes wrong."## Section 5: The Problem with Free Trials and Low Engagement
*   **The Issue:** Despite risk mitigation, free pilots still suffer from low engagement.
*   **The Fear:** Founders are frequently afraid to have conversations about willingness to pay. They worry that mentioning a dollar amount will scare the customer off.
*   **The Reality:** "But honestly, that's almost always not true. It's so so important to have the the conversation with the customer."
*   **The Key Question (Quote):** *"If I can solve this problem for you and deliver these metrics we've talked about, how much would that be worth to you?"*
*   **The Goal:** You must disqualify customers who aren't ready, able, or willing to buy your product.

## Section 6: Moving to Paid Pilots
*   **The Transition:** Once founders hit their stride and start ramping up sales, they realize they need to make pilots much shorter and get a paid commitment upfront.
*   **The Psychology of Paid Pilots:** Getting a financial commitment makes the customer take the pilot "much, much more seriously." They are now paying for your time and hopefully won't want to waste their money.
*   **Upfront Pricing Conversations:** Alongside the pilot cost, founders must ask upfront about their willingness to pay for the *full product* (e.g., annual fee, price point). "It's better to know early if they just aren't going to pay you any money whatsoever to solve this problem."
*   **Bypassing Procurement:** To avoid going through a full procurement process or having to talk to finance/the CFO, ask your champion for the amount they can personally approve.
    *   *Example:* A $10,000 or $20,000 charge on their corporate credit card.
    *   *Nuance:* The speaker would "often take less money if it allows you to shortcut a long long approval process."

## Section 7: Ensuring Pilot Success (Beyond Financial Commitment)
*   **Strategic Timing:** Wait for the right moment to start. Ask for other kinds of commitments. For example, if doing work for an auditor/accountant, wait until a live project kicks off that's suitable to test your product on.
    *   *Quote:* *"Let's not start this month. Let's wait until the first of next month because I know you've got this important new project with a new client. That would be a perfect test case for our software."*
*   **Prerequisites for Starting:** You might insist there's client data ready to go, or a person/whole team dedicated on the client side to testing your product.
*   **High Engagement:** All of that might mean delaying the start by a few weeks. But from the start date, "you can insist on a really high engagement from the customer."
*   **Rapid Iteration:** Schedule check-ins every couple of days. If they find any bugs or "niggles" with your software, you can "fix it overnight and bring it back to them, which will really impress an enterprise customer."

## Section 8: Structuring the Pilot and Time-to-Value
*   **Keep it Short:** Keep the timeframe as short as possible—"really just long enough that they can use your software and experience the full benefit." (e.g., 7 or 14 days if the product is dialed in).
*   **What You Are Actually Selling:** Because the product is so early, you're "never really selling a complete bug-free experience." You're actually selling the founders and the early team.
*   **The Founder Guarantee:** "You're selling the promise that you personally are going to solve this problem for them. You're promising that if anything goes wrong, they have your personal cell phone number and you're going to respond 24/7 to get the problem fixed."
*   **Northstar Metric - Time to First Value:** Track this like a northstar metric. Reducing it from weeks to hours is "often the biggest single lever for higher pilot to paid conversion."
*   **Doing "Janky Stuff":** "What this actually means is doing janky stuff to get your product live as soon as possible."
    *   *Example:* The speaker would *never* do a full API integration during a pilot if it requires engineering time from the customer, "because that's just going to delay you by months."
    *   *Alternative:* Work from Excel imports/exports, or ask the customer to email the data you need and email back the completed work. Get to the point of experiencing value ASAP.
*   **Post-Pilot Meeting:** Book a post-pilot meeting *before* the pilot even starts. This is where you go through the metrics at the end and show hard ROI numbers. It should be "really crystal clear" to the customer whether they want to keep using your software.

## Section 9: The "Pro Move" - Recurring Revenue Contracts with Opt-Outs
*   **The Problem with Paid Pilots:** The downside is you still need to go and negotiate the full contract afterwards. "It's like a whole second sales process just when you thought you had a customer who was ready to buy. And this can be very, very frustrating."
*   **The Solution:** Really sophisticated founders move from paid pilots to recurring revenue contracts with an opt-out period.
*   **How it Works:** This is typically a monthly or annually recurring contract with a 30 or 60-day money-back guarantee (or opt-out period) at the very start.
*   **The Magic:** By default, if the customer does nothing and is happy, it's going to turn into a full recurring contract after that opt-out period "with no additional sales process needed." "It's like magic."
*   **Positioning (Quote):** It can be very persuasive to be in a sales meeting and confidently say, *"Well, this is how customers buy our product. Typically, we offer annual contracts with a 30-day grace period or an opt-out and customer X, Y, and Z all signed on these terms."*
*   **The Caveat:** It can be hard as an early-stage founder to leap straight to this step if your sales process isn't good enough, you lack social proof, or your product isn't ready. Starting with a free pilot for the first 1-2 customers is an option. "Of course, you can't run before you can walk. But don't be stuck crawling on all fours for too long either." (Referring to overly long design partnerships).
*   **Investor Communication:** Be careful what you report to investors as MRR or ARR, especially if customers are still in that opt-out period. "Just be super clear with investors when you're reporting these numbers where the customers sit."

## Section 10: Customer Success
*   **The Next Bottleneck:** When you have early sales dialed in, you might close recurring contracts every week or two. After signing, that takes you to the next focus: customer success.
*   **The Effort:** You may often need to dedicate "as much or even more effort" to actually onboarding the customer and making sure they're getting value out of your product.
*   **The Warning:** The speaker spoke to a company that week that signed $4 million worth of contracts, but implemented less than $2 million. "They're simply missing a customer success function."

## Section 11: Rapid-Fire Sales Tips for Founders
*   **Security Certifications:** Get SOC 2 started as soon as possible, as well as others like HIPAA, ISO 27001. "These security certifications can delay you by months. And so, I'd get it started today." (Mentions companies like Vanta can help).
*   **The Internal Champion:** Figure out who your internal champion is and treat them "almost like a co-founder inside the company." This is the person who sells for you when you're not in the room and fights budget battles on your behalf.
    *   Try and set/work towards a defined closing date with them. "They're going to miss it almost every time, but it can still help create urgency within the customer."
*   **Map the Buying Process:** Ask the champion to tell you about their sales process upfront.
    *   *Quote:* *"Describe the last time you bought software like this. Who internally had to prove it? What was the process?"*
    *   Map the organization (economic buyer, technical approver, security gatekeeper, legal team, day-to-day users) and devise a plan to win over each stakeholder explicitly.
    *   Once you understand the process, try to drive it forward yourself.
*   **Always Be Closing (Next Steps):** "Don't leave a meeting without the next touch point set up."
*   **In-Person Visits:** The next tip to move a contract along is to get on a plane and physically visit your customer in person. "It can work wonders."
    *   *Tactic:* Email your champion: *"Hey, I'm going to be in Houston next week. What do you say about getting lunch?"* And if they say yes, you book your flight to Houston.
*   **Legal Redlines:** "Don't get caught up in multiple rounds of reviewing contracts or NDAs." Customer legal teams love this, but it slows you down incredibly and doesn't prevent much risk.
    *   *Advice:* Be "pretty flexible on signing, frankly, whatever your early customers want" as long as it isn't "company ending" (e.g., unlimited liability or a container clause transferring all your IP).
    *   *Mindset:* Ask yourself, "is this clause like company ending or just a slight annoyance? And frankly, put up with anything that that's not going to be company ending."
*   **Using Scarcity:** This can also work.
    *   *Quote:* *"We're talking with seven or eight potential customers, but we really only have the capacity to work with two enterprise customers this quarter. So, if you're interested, we'd love to get a commitment. Otherwise, let's talk again in 6 months."*
